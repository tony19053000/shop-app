How to Season and Care for Virgin Cast Iron

By Marcus Reed · Head of Foundry Craft · Updated Autumn 2026

Few kitchen tools possess the romantic permanence of a heavy cast iron skillet. Unlike modern non-stick pans coated with fluoropolymers that deteriorate within two or three years, bare cast iron improves in responsiveness and release performance over decades of honest use. Yet many home cooks approach the skillet with unwarranted anxiety, intimidated by archaic rules regarding dish soap, water exposure, and mysterious seasoning chemistry.

Understanding seasoning begins with demystifying what it actually is. Seasoning is not merely a coat of oil smeared across metal. It is a thin, glassy polymer layer created through a process known as oil polymerization and carbonization. When unsaturated fatty acids are heated past their smoke points in the presence of iron, they break down, link into cross-meshed polymers, and molecularly bond with the microscopic pores of the iron surface. The result is a smooth, hard, naturally hydrophobic patina that releases fried eggs, sears dry aged steaks, and protects iron from oxidation.

Selecting the Right Seasoning Oil

The most common mistake when establishing a base seasoning is using thick animal fats or low smoke-point unrefined olive oils. Lard and butter contain water and milk solids that turn rancid and scorch unevenly, while unrefined olive oil breaks down into gummy residues that flake off under spatula friction.

Instead, choose a neutral oil rich in polyunsaturated fats with a high smoke point. Refined grapeseed oil, sunflower oil, and non-GMO canola oil are ideal. Flaxseed oil was popular for several years due to its rapid drying properties, but high heat causes its brittle crystalline bonds to flake under aggressive thermal changes. Neutral grapeseed offers the optimal balance of polymer cross-linking and ductile resilience.

The Oven Method Step by Step

To establish a pristine foundation on a newly stripped or restored skillet, preheat your kitchen oven to 450°F (232°C). Wash the iron thoroughly with warm water, scrub with a stiff brush to remove any transit dust, and dry immediately with a clean cotton towel. Place the skillet on a warm stovetop burner for three minutes to evaporate every trace of moisture hidden inside the metal pores.

Pour roughly one teaspoon of grapeseed oil into the center of the warm skillet. Using a clean lint-free cloth or blue shop towel, buff the oil across the entire interior, exterior, handle, and bottom. Now take a second, completely dry towel and vigorously wipe the skillet as if you are trying to remove every drop of oil you just applied. The iron should look matte, not glossy or wet. Excess oil pooling in the corners is the primary cause of sticky, splotchy pan surfaces.

Place the skillet upside down on the middle rack of your preheated oven, with a baking sheet on the bottom rack to catch any rogue drips. Bake for 60 minutes, then turn off the oven and let the pan cool completely inside without opening the door. Repeat this procedure twice to produce three micro-thin, resilient polymer coats.

“Do not treat your iron like a museum relic. The absolute best way to build a resilient, glassy seasoning is simply cooking with it daily: searing ribeyes, roasting root vegetables, and frying pork chops.”

Everyday Cleaning and Demystifying Soap

The widespread prohibition against dish soap originated a century ago when household lye soaps contained free sodium hydroxide that stripped polymerized organic oils. Modern mild dish liquids will not harm a properly cured seasoning layer. Wash your skillet in warm water with a drop of gentle soap, rinse clean, dry with a towel, and warm it briefly on the range before wiping with three drops of oil. Stored dry, your skillet will outlive every other tool in your kitchen.

A Beginner's Guide to Precision Pour-Over

By Claire Montgomery · Specialty Beverage Director · Autumn 2026

Of all the manual methods for preparing specialty coffee, the conical pour-over dripper offers the purest window into a bean's origin, cultivar, and altitude. When dialed in with intention, a light roast washed Ethiopian coffee yields crystalline jasmine florals and sweet stone fruit notes that are often masked by the agitation and muddy immersion of French presses or automatic drip machines.

Yet the simplicity of the equipment—a ceramic cone, a paper filter, a glass carafe, and hot water—can be deceiving. Because manual percolation relies on gravity and laminar flow through a porous bed of grounds, minute adjustments in grind distribution, water temperature, and pouring cadence dramatically alter the final beverage clarity.

The Golden Ratio and Scale

Brewing exceptional coffee requires working by weight rather than volumetric spoons. Ground coffee volume varies wildly based on bean density, roast level, and moisture content; two level tablespoons can easily range between 11 and 18 grams. A simple kitchen scale reading to 0.1 grams eliminates guesswork.

We recommend a starting brew ratio of 1:16—one gram of ground coffee for every sixteen grams of brew water. For a single comforting morning mug, measure out 20 grams of whole bean coffee and 320 grams of filtered water heated to 204°F (95°C). If your roast is particularly dark, drop the temperature to 198°F to prevent extracting harsh, bitter tannins; for delicate ultra-light roasts, boil right off the flame at 208°F.

Rinsing the Filter and The Bloom Phase

Place your paper cone filter into the stoneware dripper and rinse it thoroughly with 150 ml of hot water. This accomplishes two essential functions: it washes away residual lignins and papery flavors, and it preheats the dense ceramic walls of the dripper so they don't siphon thermal energy from your slurry during extraction. Discard the rinse water before adding grounds.

Grind your coffee to the consistency of coarse kosher salt. Shake the dripper gently to level the dry bed, zero your scale, and start your timer. Pour 60 grams of hot water in gentle concentric circles, ensuring all dry coffee grounds are saturated.

This is the bloom phase. For the next 45 seconds, roasted coffee violently releases trapped carbon dioxide gas, visibly bubbling like a miniature volcano. Allowing the gas to escape unrestricted ensures that subsequent pour water can penetrate the cellular structure of the bean instead of channeling around gas barriers.

“Focus your pour entirely in gentle concentric circles within the center circle of the coffee bed. Never wash water directly down the paper wall of the dripper, which bypasses the grounds entirely.”

The Pour Cadence and Drawdown

At 0:45, begin your first main pulse with your gooseneck kettle. Pour a slow, steady stream from a height of three inches, maintaining a spiral from the center outward to about an inch from the rim. Add 140 grams of water (bringing the scale to 200g) by 1:15. Pause twenty seconds to allow the liquid level to drop halfway.

At 1:35, pour the remaining 120 grams of water with steady, calm revolutions until your scale hits the final 320-gram target. Give the dripper one gentle horizontal swirl to settle any grounds clinging to the sidewalls. The water should drain through in a smooth, flat bed of grounds by 3:00 to 3:30. Swirl your glass carafe to aerate the brew, pour into a hand-thrown ceramic mug, and savor the slow morning clarity.

Caring for Handcrafted Stoneware & Natural Glazes

By Julian Vance · Studio Potter · Autumn 2026

There is an unmistakable vitality in dinnerware thrown by hand from natural clay. Unlike mass-manufactured vitrified china stamped from sterile paste, handcrafted stoneware celebrates organic variation: iron specks rising through the clay body, glaze pooling in subtle gradations, and rims that follow the rhythm of a potter's wheel.

Stoneware is fired at temperatures exceeding 2,200°F (1,200°C), fusing clay minerals into a dense, stone-like matrix impervious to liquid absorption. When cared for thoughtfully, handmade plates, fluted pasta bowls, and ceramic mugs easily endure generations of bustling dinners. Here are three essential tenets for keeping your artisan tableware pristine.

Navigating Thermal Shock

While high-fire stoneware is remarkably robust, ceramic expands and contracts when exposed to rapid shifts in temperature. This thermal gradient creates stress within the ceramic matrix known as thermal shock, which can induce micro-fractures or hairline crazing across the glaze.

To protect your plates and bowls, never transfer stoneware straight from a cold refrigerator into a 400°F oven or microwave. Allow cold ceramics to reach room temperature on the kitchen counter for ten minutes before reheating food. Similarly, avoid placing a piping hot dinner plate straight off the stove onto a freezing cold quartz or granite countertop; rest it on a wooden trivet or linen runner to buffer the temperature transition.

Preventing and Removing Cutlery Marks

A frequent question from new stoneware owners concerns light gray streaks that occasionally appear on satin matte glazes after cutting steak or crusty bread. Many assume the metal knife has scratched away the ceramic glaze, but the opposite is true: high-fire glaze is significantly harder than standard flatware steel.

What you are observing is actually a microscopic deposit of metal left behind on the micro-textured surface of the matte glaze—analogous to graphite from a pencil marking paper. These streaks do not harm the glaze and can be easily removed.

Sprinkle a small pinch of Bar Keepers Friend powder or baking soda onto the marked plate, add a few drops of water to create a creamy paste, and buff gently with a soft sponge or damp Swedish dishcloth. Within thirty seconds, the metal deposits dissolve completely, restoring the silky satin glaze to its original unblemished state.

“Handmade ceramics are made to be lived with, not locked behind glass. The gentle wear of shared dinners and Sunday breakfasts only adds to the narrative of honest tableware.”

Dishwasher Best Practices

All Kettle & Crate stoneware is dishwasher safe. However, the abrasive environment inside a crowded dishwasher requires attentive loading. Always space handcrafted ceramic plates and bowls so they do not vibrate or clatter against heavy cast iron or stainless steel utensils during high-pressure wash cycles. Use mild, chlorine-free detergents that preserve the soft tactile sheen of mineral glazes, and open the dishwasher door slightly after the cycle to allow steam to disperse naturally.
